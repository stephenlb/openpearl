What we learned from giving an autonomous coding pipeline 63 tickets

We wanted to find out how much a ticket-to-PR pipeline could handle on its own while building a small project. Could it recover from failures, improve its workflow and tune itself as it went? We opened 63 GitHub issues with gh issue create and gave OpenPearl room to work. Fifty tickets described a new product named pearl-engine, a zero-dependency Node.js library that retries, heals, learns and tunes itself. The rest covered pipeline fixes, re-filed failures and a chaos drill. We learned a lot along the way, including where the pipeline surprised us and where it still appreciated a helping hand.

The short version

The encouraging part is that the engine did well in simulation, and the pipeline became dependable at recovery once we showed it what to do. All 50 engine deliverables reached main, although we had to re-file 4 of them. The pipeline also merged 8 fixes to its own gates and workflows. A person still had to notice and request each new kind of fix, while its only autonomous trigger handled red tests on main. The five challenge tickets helped us see that balance more clearly: the pipeline resolved simultaneous edits, made a reasonable guess on an ambiguous request, left a test-first requirement unverifiable, and returned honest diagnostics when a task could not succeed as written.

We were happy to see 59 of 63 PRs land. We also noticed that 37 of 62 PRs merged while the test gate reported fail. The pipeline kept moving, but our main quality signal was no longer helping us tell a healthy PR from a troubled one. That became an especially useful lesson from the run.

How we set up the experiment

Here is the setup for anyone who would like to follow the path we took or try a similar run.

  • Target: research/engine, ESM, no dependencies, tests with node:test, every module small and deterministic (injected clock and RNG).
  • 50 tickets in 6 phases: 10 core, 10 self-healing, 10 self-improving, 10 self-optimizing, 5 measurement, 5 challenge tickets (an ambiguous request, a seeded bug, two simultaneous edits of the same file and a request that could not succeed as written).
  • Dispatch: issues were opened in waves (3 at a time, then 2 after the rate-limit incident). A wave never contained a ticket that depends on another in the same wave, except the deliberate conflicting pair E48/E49.
  • Pipeline under test: unmodified issue-to-pr.yml with Claude Code on Bedrock, 2 review rounds, AUTO_MERGE=1 (eval and guards skipped).
  • Metrics: research/harness/collect.py reads runs, issues, PRs and comments through gh. engine_eval.mjs measures the product. charts.py and report.py produce this page.

The pipeline kept moving steadily

Each bar follows a ticket from dispatch to the end of its workflow. The fastest run took 106 seconds, and the median took 201. The longest run also had a clear story behind it: E49 edited the same file as E48 and then took extra time to reconcile the two changes. E33 (416 seconds) and E40 (349 seconds) were the next slowest.

Most runs were fairly predictable. Nineteen finished in under 150 seconds, and 38 took 150 to 300 seconds. Only 6 crossed five minutes. That kept the mean (205 seconds) close to the median (201), despite a p95 of 316 seconds.

The pipeline did slow down as the experiment continued. Mean run time rose from 190 seconds for the first 25 experiment tickets to 234 seconds for the last 25, a 23% increase. The growing codebase may explain some of that, although we did not isolate the cause. The pipeline did not adjust its settings in response, so self-optimizing remains an area to explore.

We opened the issues over 3.7 hours in waves of three, then two after the rate-limit incident. That kept the queue short enough for merges to follow dispatch closely. The line flattened when four runs died at step 1 over about 40 minutes and when four PRs lost merge races and had to be re-filed. Even with those stalls, 59 of 63 PRs merged, including 46 of 50 experiment PRs on their first attempt.

The optimize phase produced the largest PRs (156 added lines on average) and the slowest runs (241 seconds), while heal tickets averaged 140 lines and finished fastest at 177 seconds. The challenge tickets averaged only 47 lines and still took 232 seconds. Conflict handling, review and workflow retries mattered alongside diff size.

The PRs that needed another attempt clustered in core (9/10 merged) and heal (8/10); optimize, measure and challenge all reached 100%. All 13 pipeline-fix tickets merged too. Those fixes were the smallest PRs on average, yet drew the most review findings (15). That makes sense in context: they touched workflow files, where even a small diff can shape every run that follows.

The pipeline resolved all 17 merge conflicts without help. After F02 added a merge-retry loop, all 3 later merge races recovered. After F07 added the watchdog, both later red-main events recovered. The incidents that still needed us happened before a recovery path existed: 4 early merge races, 4 runs interrupted by rate limits and a test gate that was either blind or already red.

Each mark represents a PR in merge order. The opening none marks appear because there was no root test script until F01. Once a failing test reached main, every later PR inherited it and received fail, regardless of what that PR changed. F04 and F06 briefly restored green. This made the signal hard to use: a red mark did not necessarily mean its PR introduced a problem, and a gate that stayed red could not point us to the PR that did.

There was another gap in the test evidence. In 29 of 63 PR summaries, the agent says it did not run the tests. The gate was our only independent check, and it was unavailable until F01 and red for much of the run. We would want a stronger safety net before relying on this workflow for less forgiving work.

The pipeline recovered from 22 of 34 incidents on its own (65%), with familiar conflicts accounting for 17 of those 22 recoveries. Without conflicts, the rate was 5 of 17 (29%). We came away confident that the pipeline could follow recovery paths it knew, while unfamiliar failures still benefited from a person stepping in.

Most of the manual recovery time was spent waiting for someone to notice, not repairing the problem itself. The first watchdog event took 7.5 minutes, including 343 seconds that passed before the watchdog existed. In the chaos drill, detection took 13 seconds and the repair took 93. Giving the pipeline a way to notice the problem made the biggest difference.

Five tickets that invited a little more judgment

For the last five experiment tickets, we gave the pipeline a few less tidy situations: ambiguity, a seeded bug with a process requirement, simultaneous edits to the same function, and an impossible request. All five merged, and each gave us a different view of how the pipeline makes decisions.

E46 offers a useful lesson for everyday product work. The chosen optimization is reasonable: a non-adaptive cache does not need per-key hit and miss history. Still, “faster” could have meant task latency, throughput, memory use or startup time. The pipeline chose cache bookkeeping, and its new test checked behavior rather than measuring elapsed time. The patch was tidy, but a quick clarifying question would have helped it solve the right product problem.

E47 separates code correctness from process compliance. The final diff covers the seeded edge cases and fixes the cap, but the ticket also required a failing test first. With a single commit and an admission that tests were not run, the experiment cannot show that the pipeline followed the requested red-green sequence.

E48 and E49 gave us the clearest success in the set. Both tickets changed the same function at nearly the same time. E48 landed onGiveUp first; E49 then resolved the conflict, retained that callback and added timeout handling. E50 also produced a good result, with the prompt offering a path away from the request that could not be fulfilled literally. A useful next step would be helping the pipeline ask a question or invite a human in when a ticket leaves room for interpretation.

The engine gave us more encouraging results in simulation

By the end of the run, the engine had grown to 43 source files, 2,474 source lines and 3,585 test lines, with 392 passing tests. This is where the experiment began to feel especially promising.

At 10 to 30% injected faults, healing lifts success to 99% or better. At 60%, it more than doubles success from 41.1% to 87.9%, the largest gain in the experiment. At 90% faults, only 34.5% of jobs succeed because repeated attempts keep hitting the same high failure probability. Variation across seeds also widens as faults rise.

That extra reliability comes with a tradeoff. At 30% faults, mean attempts rise from 1.00 to 1.40 and p95 latency rises from 10 to 40 virtual milliseconds. By 60%, jobs average 2.16 attempts. Tail latency flattens at 55 because the retry budget caps it, not because the dependency has recovered. In production, we would pair those retries with care so they do not add too much load to a dependency that is already struggling.

Regret measures the reward lost when the selector does not choose the best option. Random selection keeps making the same class of mistake, so its regret climbs roughly linearly. Thompson sampling learns faster: it cuts regret by 94% against random and chooses the best arm 95% of the time.

At 800 samples per arm, the gate detects a true 10-point lift 99% of the time and falsely adopts an equal arm 2% of the time. At 25 samples per arm, it detects the same lift only 13% of the time. For this pipeline experiment, that means 50 tickets do not provide enough observations to tune settings safely with this gate.

The tuner takes a little time to get going between 5 and 10 evaluations, then closes quickly on the optimum. Random search improves more slowly and remains farther away, even with four times the budget.

At the smallest budget, the tuner is already about twice as close as random search (3.38 versus 6.13). At 160 evaluations it is 31 times closer. These are averages over 30 seeds on a synthetic three-parameter problem. They show that the algorithm works under these test conditions, but they do not tell us how much it would improve this pipeline.

The fixed 250-millisecond threshold treats nearly every slower call after the shift as a timeout. The adaptive threshold follows the new distribution and settles at a mean of 513 milliseconds. That removes 466 false timeouts, but a dead call now takes about twice as long to declare. Adaptation trades faster failure detection for fewer false alarms.

The limiter does not know the safe concurrency ahead of time. It increases until requests fail, then backs off. When simulated capacity drops from 24 to 8, concurrency follows it closely enough to keep rejection at 1.21% across the run. This is the behavior we expect from AIMD, so the result gives us confidence in the implementation under simulation rather than revealing a new algorithmic result.

The overall score is 87, but it is not an external grade. We chose how each measurement maps into the engine's scorecard(). The chart helps compare the three areas under that mapping; it does not mean the engine is “87% self-improving” in a general sense.

Adaptive TTL raised the hit rate from 16.1% to 17.9% on a skewed 5,000-request workload.

scenarios.json lists 12 scenarios, but the simulator models only failure rate. The latency-spike and corruption scenarios therefore reproduce the plain fault-rate results, leaving 8 distinct outcomes. Ticket E44 asked for 12 scenarios, and the pipeline followed the request closely. A stronger check of the simulator's capabilities would have helped it notice that all 12 could not yet be represented.

What we took away from the run

Self-healing

The pipeline heals failures covered by existing rules: conflicts (17/17), merge races after F02 (3/3) and red tests on main after F07 (2/2). New kinds of failure still need a person to help establish the recovery path. The rate-limit incident needed 8 manual reruns, and the comment-chat agent cannot run git fetch/merge, so it could not rescue stale PRs. F05 and F08 target those situations but were never exercised afterward. For now, healing means a growing collection of hand-reviewed rules, and the run gave us a useful foundation to build on.

Self-improving

The pipeline implemented all 8 requested fixes, including four edits to its own workflow files. We still chose what to improve, but once asked, it handled the implementation well. Its autonomous improvement path is currently the watchdog, which turns a red npm test into a ticket. Connecting failed runs, slow runs and repeated review findings to the same loop would be a natural next step. The engine already has a module for that, issuedraft.js, waiting to be connected to the pipeline.

Self-optimizing

The pipeline did not change its own parameters during this run. Review rounds, diff limits, wave size and concurrency were fixed or hand-set, and we lowered the wave size after the rate-limit incident. Run time grew along with the repo. The good news is that the engine already contains tuners and adaptive components that performed as designed in simulation; the next challenge is giving the pipeline a safe way to use them.

A few things to keep in mind

We want to keep the results in perspective. This was a useful experiment, not a final verdict on what the pipeline can do.

  • A single run with a single model and no repeats. Timings include GitHub queueing and the rate-limit incident; no confidence intervals for pipeline metrics.
  • The same model family writes the code, reviews it and judges it, with AUTO_MERGE=1 bypassing the independent eval. Test pass rates here do not show that the code is correct.
  • The engine is evaluated in simulation with synthetic faults and virtual time; production behavior is untested.
  • The scorecard maps measurements to its inputs by our own choice (for example A/B power stands in for regression coverage). Treat the 0-100 numbers as illustrative.
  • Operator interventions are part of the data: 8 manual reruns, 5 rate-limit probe comments, 1 chat nudge, 1 PR-merge attempt that was blocked, and the choice of which findings became fix tickets.
  • The incident ledger counts are our classification.

What we'd be excited to try next

If we run the experiment again, these are the changes we would start with.

  1. Pair AUTO_MERGE=1 with a test gate that blocks when the gate says fail. In this run, 37 PRs merged red.
  2. Keep F07 and extend the watchdog to failed workflow runs, so F05/F08-style incidents open tickets too.
  3. Serialize or queue merges, or keep shared registries such as index.js out of tickets: that file caused the original merge races.
  4. Feed run metrics (duration, retries, findings per PR) back into settings such as review rounds and wave size, with an A/B gate like the engine's compareArms.
  5. Give the agent a way to ask about under-specified tickets before choosing an interpretation (E46).
